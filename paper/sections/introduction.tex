\section{Introduction}

Automated diagnostic systems deployed in dermatology routinely encounter
images acquired under conditions their training data does not represent:
different capture devices, illumination, populations, and clinical settings.
Because a classifier's confidence is unreliable on such inputs, deployed
systems commonly pair the classifier with a separate safeguard that estimates
whether an input falls outside the training distribution. Distance-based
estimators---Mahalanobis distance to class centroids and its
relatives---remain the dominant choice for this role, and they rest on a
geometric premise: that inputs from an unseen acquisition domain occupy a
measurably different region of the representation space than the training
population does.

Two lines of evidence complicate that premise, and they have so far remained
separate.

The first concerns whether the premise holds at all. On convolutional
representations of skin lesions, a linear probe recovered acquisition-domain
identity at high accuracy---establishing that the domain signal was present
and linearly decodable---while distance-based estimators operating on the
same representations performed near chance~\cite{tran2026decodable}. The
information distinguishing the domains was decodable, but not accessible to
the estimator intended to exploit it. This dissociation is clinically
relevant because it describes a failure mode in which the safeguard reports
normality precisely where a probe could establish that the input is
anomalous.

The second concerns what governs estimator performance. Across a range of
frozen vision foundation-model backbones, two properties of the
in-distribution representation---its local intrinsic dimensionality (LID) and
the decay profile of its within-class covariance spectrum---were found to
track how well Mahalanobis-style estimators perform~\cite{yu2510dissecting}.
That work reports an association measured across representations produced by
different pretraining regimes, and characterizes it as tracking rather than
causation.

Whether the second line of evidence explains the first is an open question,
and it is the question we take up. An association observed across
representations is consistent with several readings: geometry may govern what
distance-based estimators can recover; or both geometry and estimator
performance may follow jointly from properties of the pretraining regime that
distinguish one backbone from another. These readings are not separable by
comparing backbones, because backbones differ in many respects at once.
Distinguishing them requires holding the representation's provenance fixed
and intervening on geometry directly---and if geometry governs accessibility
in the sense the dissociation would require, such an intervention should
change what distance-based estimators recover from a representation whose
domain content is otherwise preserved.

We examined where that hypothesis can be evaluated, on a frozen dermatology
foundation model, within a single fixed backbone rather than across backbones.
Rather than proposing a new out-of-distribution detector, we characterised the
conditions an explanatory reading requires,
under a preregistered protocol incorporating implementation-integrity
verification, manipulation checking, and prespecified statistical analysis
with multiplicity correction fixed in advance of execution. Only checkpoints
clearing both verification stages enter the causal comparison.

Our findings delimit where such a reading can be evaluated at all, under the
evaluated backbone, layer, and task, rather than adjudicating it.
The probe--reliability dissociation remained statistically detectable, but at
approximately two orders of magnitude smaller a margin than originally
reported, with distance-based estimators already operating near ceiling on
the unmodified representation. The two preregistered geometry metrics showed
no Holm-significant association with estimator performance. These results
indicate that associations observed across representations warrant direct
intervention-based evaluation before being interpreted as explanatory.

This study is organised around three questions, fixed before execution.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Can targeted geometric properties be systematically
        manipulated while preserving task utility and domain information?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] Within a fixed frozen backbone, do changes in
        preregistered geometry metrics predict changes in distance-based OOD
        ranking?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] When geometry is matched across nominally different
        intervention configurations, does OOD performance follow geometry or
        intervention configuration?
\end{itemize}

The contributions are three.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A scorer-relevant invariance formalization.} We separate raw
        representation geometry, the geometry a scorer actually reads, score
        magnitude, cross-domain ranking, and AUROC, which the literature
        commonly treats as one chain. A geometry metric earns interpretation as
        a predictor of ranking only after the first two are shown to coincide.
        We supply that step for the operator used here
        (Section~\ref{sec:invariance}): it sets the condition number in closed
        form while leaving Mahalanobis scoring exactly invariant, so one of the
        four estimators is structurally unable to respond to it, and three are
        not.
  \item \textbf{A gated within-backbone intervention protocol.} Cross-backbone
        studies vary architecture, pretraining corpus, objective, dimension,
        and feature layer together; when geometry and performance move
        together there, geometry may be an explanation or merely a correlate of
        backbone provenance. We hold backbone, extraction pipeline, feature
        layer, splits, evaluation protocol, and scorer definitions fixed, and
        intervene on the representation output alone, behind an
        implementation-integrity gate and an explicit manipulation check.
  \item \textbf{Empirical boundary evidence in a near-ceiling regime.} The
        contribution is not that geometry fails to predict AUROC. It is a
        characterisation of where the question can be asked: domain information
        and task utility survive the intervention and the targeted geometry
        moves, so a trivial explanation is excluded; the preregistered metrics
        nonetheless do not account for ranking in the evaluated configuration;
        and baseline performance above 0.99 for every estimator bounds what any
        such account could have been observed to explain here.
\end{itemize}
